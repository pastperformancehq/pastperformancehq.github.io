Notes

  • The definition of a bubble used here follows Brunnermeier and Oehmke (2012), p. 25: the part of the price above even the most optimistic buyer’s valuation. Scheinkman and Xiong (2003), p. 1186 n. 7, measure it from each owner’s own number instead. The multiplying form of the sketch is ours; the papers support the three drivers, not the product.
  • “The price is the keenest buyer’s number, plus the bubble” assumes the keenest buyers can hold the whole supply. When their money runs short, the price stays below the most optimistic valuation (Brunnermeier and Oehmke, p. 26).
  • “No more than sixteen per cent” is Garber’s figure, and his rates compound continuously. Compounded annually, it is about 22 per cent.
  • Mackay’s anecdotes and the 1637 dialogues are satire and moral tale. They are reported here as what those authors said, not as fact.

Credit for ideas

  • The narrator’s view that a bubble is a bet on the next buyer follows the resale-option literature: Robert J. Shiller, Irrational Exuberance (3rd ed., 2015), p. 264; Brunnermeier and Oehmke, “Bubbles, Financial Crises, and Systemic Risk” (2012), p. 25.
